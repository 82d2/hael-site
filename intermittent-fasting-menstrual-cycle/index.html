intermittent fasting and the menstrual cycle

by Joy, who makes hæl · october 12, 2026

the short answer

small trials of daily fasting in women have mostly not found harm to reproductive hormones, but they are short and few. the real risk to the cycle is eating too little overall, which can stop periods. shorter fasts late in the cycle are a sensible choice; no study proves they're required.

does intermittent fasting affect your period?

in the trials so far, mostly not in the ways people fear. a 2022 review of human studies found that fasting didn't clearly change estrogen or the pituitary hormones that drive ovulation. androgens such as testosterone fell in premenopausal women with obesity, which may help in polycystic ovary syndrome.1 in an eight-week trial of eating within four to six hours a day, most sex hormones held steady; DHEA, an adrenal hormone, fell by about a seventh and stayed within the normal range.2

the honest caveat is how thin this is. the trials are small, short, and mostly in women with obesity. almost none followed lean women with regular cycles through several months, and none tested fasting timed to the phases of the cycle.

can fasting stop your period?

yes, if it adds up to too little food. the body treats a long shortfall of energy, especially with heavy exercise, stress, or weight loss, as a reason to pause ovulation. doctors call this functional hypothalamic amenorrhea.3 the risk comes from eating too little overall, not from the clock: a fast followed by enough food is a different thing from a fast that becomes a way of eating less and less.

a missed period, cycles getting much longer, or bleeding stopping for three months or more is a reason to stop fasting and see a doctor. so is a fast that starts to feel like something you can't stop.

can you fast during your period?

there's no evidence that a fast is harmful during a period for a healthy woman. what changes is how it feels. cramps, low energy, and poor sleep make any fast harder, and a heavy period is a good reason to eat enough iron. a shorter fast, or none, on the first heavy days is a reasonable choice; so is your usual one, if it feels fine.

should you fast less in the luteal phase?

this is the core of "cycle syncing", and no trial has tested it. what is known: resting metabolism may rise a little after ovulation, though newer studies find the effect small,4 and many women feel hungrier and less patient in the week before a period. a long fast in those days is likely to feel harder.

so shortening a fast in the late luteal phase is a sensible way to keep the practice easy to live with. it isn't a medical rule, and anyone selling it as one, in either direction, is overstating the evidence.

what is cycle syncing, for fasting?

it means matching the length of a fast to the phase of the cycle: longer in the follicular phase, between the period and ovulation, when many women find fasting easier; shorter in the days before a period. it is a practical idea built on how the cycle feels, not on proven hormonal effects. treat it as a way to listen, not a prescription.

is intermittent fasting safe for women?

for most healthy adult women, shorter daily fasts such as 12 to 16 hours appear to be well tolerated in studies so far. some women should not fast without a doctor's advice:

  • during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or while trying to conceive
  • with a current or past eating disorder
  • with diabetes, or on medicines that lower blood sugar
  • under 18
  • with a missed or irregular period that hasn't been looked into

does any of this apply on birth control?

hormonal birth control that stops ovulation also flattens the phases, so there is no luteal phase to time a fast around. fasting by the hours alone, and by how you feel, makes more sense than following a cycle chart.
