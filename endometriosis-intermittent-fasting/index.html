endometriosis and intermittent fasting

by the maker of hæl · november 6, 2026

the short answer

endometriosis affects about 1 in 10 women of reproductive age, and diagnosis often takes years. no trial has tested intermittent fasting in it; the only related studies cut calories in mice. the European guideline makes no recommendation on diet because the evidence is unclear. on days of pain, fatigue or nausea, a shorter fast or none is a reasonable choice; treatment is for a doctor.

hæl, a fasting timer for iPhone, suggests its shortest hours while you bleed and keeps a day of rest as rest, never as a gap.

what is endometriosis?

endometriosis is a long-term condition in which tissue like the lining of the womb grows outside it, causing inflammation and scar tissue. the World Health Organization estimates that it affects about 10% of women of reproductive age worldwide, some 190 million. it lists severe period pain, heavy bleeding, chronic pelvic pain that doesn't go away when the period ends, infertility, and bloating and nausea among the symptoms. there is no cure; treatment aims to control symptoms.1

diagnosis is slow. the WHO puts the average wait at between 4 and 12 years.1 in a study of 1,418 women having laparoscopy in ten countries, the delay from first symptoms to a surgical diagnosis was 6.7 years, mostly in primary care, and women with endometriosis lost on average 10.8 hours of work a week, mainly from working less well while in pain.2 in the UK, Endometriosis UK gives the average time from a first GP visit to diagnosis as 9 years and 4 months.3

what do doctors recommend?

the European guideline, from ESHRE in 2022, and the UK's NICE guideline agree on the outline.45

  • pain relief. NICE suggests a short trial, for example three months, of paracetamol or an anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen, alone or together, as a first step.5 ESHRE says these may be offered, as a weak recommendation.4
  • hormonal treatment, such as the combined pill, a progestogen, a hormonal coil or implant, or medicines that pause the cycle. both guidelines recommend it as an option for pain.45
  • surgery, as one of the options for pain, with NICE asking doctors to be guided by a person's symptoms, priorities and wishes about fertility.45

NICE also advises that keeping a pain and symptom diary can help conversations with a doctor.5 these are things to talk through with a doctor; nothing on this page replaces them.

what do the guidelines say about diet?

very little, because the evidence is thin. ESHRE asks doctors to discuss non-medical ways of coping, and then says that no recommendation can be made for any specific one, nutrition included, to reduce pain or improve quality of life, "as the potential benefits and harms are unclear".4 it suggests a healthy lifestyle and diet in general terms, not as a treatment.4 NICE makes no recommendation on diet at all; its one non-drug recommendation is that the evidence does not support Chinese medicine or supplements for treating endometriosis.5 neither guideline mentions fasting.

what is known about diet and endometriosis?

mostly observational studies, a few small trials, and surveys.

  • in the Nurses' Health Study II, which followed women for 12 years, those who ate the most long-chain omega-3 fats were 22% less likely to be diagnosed with endometriosis than those who ate the least, and those who ate the most trans fats were 48% more likely.6 that is about risk of diagnosis, not about symptoms, and it can't show cause.
  • a 2022 systematic review found nine human studies of a dietary change in endometriosis, each testing a different one. most reported a benefit, but all had a moderate or high risk of bias.7
  • in an Australian survey of 484 women with surgically confirmed endometriosis, 76% used some form of self-management. the commonest were heat, 70%, and rest, 68%; 44% had changed their diet, and they rated it 6.4 out of 10 for pain relief, with no one diet doing better than another.8 a second paper from the same survey found that cutting gluten, cutting dairy and the low-FODMAP diet were the commonest changes.9

the best-studied diet is low-FODMAP, which cuts certain fermentable carbohydrates for a few weeks and then brings them back one by one. among 160 women with irritable bowel syndrome at a New Zealand clinic, 36% also had endometriosis, and 72% of them reported bowel symptoms more than halved after four weeks on the diet, against 49% of those with IBS alone.10 in 2025 a randomised crossover trial fed 35 women with endometriosis and gut symptoms a low-FODMAP diet and a control diet for 28 days each: 60% responded to the low-FODMAP diet and 26% to the control. pain in the abdomen, bloating and quality of life improved; tiredness and nausea did not.11 it is a gut treatment, tested on gut symptoms.

the charities are careful. Endometriosis UK says there is little high-quality evidence that particular diets help, that low-FODMAP may offer some relief alongside IBS-like symptoms, and that it should be done with a registered dietitian.12 Endometriosis Australia says the same diet may help those with IBS or IBS-like symptoms, works best short term, and is worth doing with professional support.13

restriction is already common. in a UK survey of 1,385 people with endometriosis, 36.7% were eating a restricted diet, 29.8% had a decreased appetite, and 13.5% had recently lost weight without meaning to.14

what people with endometriosis have told researchers

in a Swedish study, twelve people with endometriosis who had changed their diet were interviewed. most had cut or reduced gluten, dairy or carbohydrates, and they described less pain and more energy; one said a general low-FODMAP diet hadn't helped and changed course. they were recruited from online forums linked to diet, so they speak for people for whom a change seemed to work. one said, in the published translation, "…everybody reacts differently to diet…I think that you need to know your body and sense whether something makes you feel good or bad…". another described raising it with health services: "It’s just like meeting a brick wall, you just get a: ‘Nah, it’s meaningless, just eat what you want’."15 none of them described fasting.

has intermittent fasting been studied in endometriosis?

no. as of november 2026, searches of PubMed, Europe PMC and ClinicalTrials.gov found no trial of intermittent fasting, time-restricted eating or meal timing in people with endometriosis, and no registered one. there is no evidence that fasting helps endometriosis, and none that it harms it. there is simply no study.

the nearest work is in mice, and it isn't fasting. in one study, mice fed 30% fewer calories than mice eating freely grew much smaller endometriosis lesions.16 in another, mice on a calorie-restricted diet for 90 days weighed 24.5% less and had fewer, lighter lesions.17 both tested eating less every day, not eating within a window, in animals with endometriosis induced by surgery or injection. they say nothing about what a fast does to pain in a person.

pain, fatigue and nausea

these are the reasons a fast may not suit some days.

  • pain. in endometriosis it often comes with the period, and for some it is there on other days too.1 on a day of bad pain, a long fast adds a hard thing to a hard day.
  • fatigue. in a study of 560 women with endometriosis and 560 without, frequent fatigue was reported by 50.7% of those with it and 22.4% of those without; it went with insomnia, depression and pain.18 in the UK survey, tiredness was the commonest symptom, reported by 85.5%, ahead of bloating, 75.3%, and abdominal pain, 67.3%.14
  • nausea. the WHO lists it among the symptoms.1 in the Australian survey, some who changed their diet reported less nausea and vomiting,9 but in the low-FODMAP trial nausea didn't improve.11
  • pain relief. the NHS advises taking ibuprofen with or after food, and lists indigestion and stomach ulcers among its side effects.19 if you take an anti-inflammatory on painful days, a fast that leaves you taking it on an empty stomach is worth asking a pharmacist about.

rest, in the Australian survey, meant physical rest, and women rated it 5.3 out of 10 for pain.8 it is not the same as a day without a fast, and no study has measured that.

who shouldn't fast

  • anyone with a current or past eating disorder. a 2026 review reports a large genetic study finding nearly three times the odds of eating disorders in women with endometriosis, and says disordered eating, including restrictive dieting, is common and tied to pain.20 NHS guidance says people with a history of eating disorders shouldn't try intermittent fasting.21
  • during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or while trying to conceive.2122 infertility is part of endometriosis for many,1 and eating around fertility treatment is for a doctor or midwife, not a timer.
  • anyone with diabetes on insulin or a sulfonylurea, which can cause low blood sugar without food; doses need a doctor's guidance on fasting days.23
  • anyone under 18, or with weight loss, a poor appetite or a missed period that hasn't been looked into.
  • when fasting starts to feel like a way to control pain by eating less, or like something you can't stop.

if you are being treated for endometriosis, mention fasting to whoever prescribes for you.

where hæl fits

hæl doesn't treat endometriosis, and no study shows that fasting does. what it can do is keep the hours from asking for more on the days you have less. with the full witness, it suggests twelve to fourteen hours while you bleed, the shortest of the cycle, and fourteen to sixteen in the luteal phase, from ovulation to the period. its line for the period reads, "a rest day counts as part of the practice." that isn't a finding about endometriosis. it's a way of making the hard days easy to step back from.

two honest limits. hæl counts the period as the first five days of the cycle, whatever its real length, so pain that starts before bleeding, or lasts beyond it, falls under the luteal or follicular hours. and if hormonal treatment has stopped your periods, the phases don't describe you, and turning the cycle off is the truer choice.

setting hæl up around painful days

  1. tell hæl about your cycle, or don't. on the first page, give the day your last period began, read it from Apple Health, or choose "I don't keep one". if your periods have stopped with treatment, turn off "track a cycle" in settings: the ring keeps the hours alone, and the ledger draws the month by the moon, as a calendar only.
  2. let the dial sit lower. with the full witness, hæl suggests thirteen hours during the period, written under the dial; tap it to set the dial there. on any day, turn the dial lower, down to eight hours. if you'd rather the cycle didn't shape the suggestion, turn off "let the cycle shape the suggestion" in settings.
  3. press rest. on a day you won't fast, press rest under begin. the screen says "resting today." and "the practice keeps no score against it." rest days are free, and they don't break your days in a row; with "cycle-aware streaks" on, neither do period days. you can mark today only, so decide on the morning, not the week before.
  4. end a fast when you need to. press end fast, then end. the fast is kept as it was, at whatever length it reached. if you'd rather it weren't kept, choose discard this fast.
  5. keep your pain diary elsewhere. NICE suggests one for talking to a doctor;5 hæl observes fasts, not symptoms, so keep it on paper or in an app made for it.

the timer, the ledger and rest days are free. the hours for each phase are part of the full witness. how hæl reads the cycle.

hæl doesn't treat endometriosis, and neither does fasting. it suggests shorter hours while you bleed, keeps a day of rest as rest, and lets you end any fast without a word against it. for endometriosis itself, see a doctor; Endometriosis UK is a good place to start.
