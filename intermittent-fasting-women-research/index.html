intermittent fasting for women: the research gap

by the maker of hæl · november 6, 2026

the short answer

women were often in fasting trials, but results were rarely reported by sex, and the menstrual cycle was rarely recorded. small trials in women found reproductive hormones mostly unchanged; the fear traces largely to a study in young rats. no trial has tested fasting by phase of the cycle. eating too little can stop periods, and some people shouldn't fast at all.

hæl, a fasting timer for iPhone, suggests hours for each phase of the cycle and keeps them suggestions, because no trial has tested them.

were women left out of the research?

for a long time, often. in 1977 the US Food and Drug Administration advised keeping women who could become pregnant out of early drug studies, and it reversed that advice in 1993. the same year, the NIH Revitalization Act, signed on June 10, 1993, required research funded by the National Institutes of Health to include women and, in clinical trials, to be designed for "a valid analysis" of whether the treatment affects women differently. in practice the NIH applied the analysis requirement to phase 3 trials only.1

the law covered people, not laboratory animals or cells. in June 2015 the NIH added that it expects sex as a biological variable to be "factored into research designs, analyses, and reporting in vertebrate animal and human studies", for grant applications from January 25, 2016.2

including women and analysing by sex are two different things, and the second has moved more slowly. a survey of papers in 34 journals found that more studies used both sexes in 2019 than in 2009, but of 356 papers that did, only 42% analysed the data by sex, against 50% ten years earlier. most gave no reason for leaving sex out, and those that did often leaned on what the authors call misconceptions about female hormonal variability.3

does that apply to diet and metabolism?

it depends on the kind of study. a machine reading of 43,135 published clinical studies found women under-represented in 7 of 11 disease groups. it was worst in some fields, such as heart disease, where 37% of participants per study were women against a 51% share of the disease; in diabetes it was close, 45% against 48%.4

weight-loss trials run the other way. across 244 randomised trials of diet and lifestyle published from 1999 to 2009, participants were 73% women, and 32% of the trials enrolled only women.5 so the gap for women in fasting research isn't mostly a matter of headcount. it's in which studies they were part of, whether results were reported by sex, and whether anyone recorded the cycle.

what about the fasting trials themselves?

some of the most cited early trials enrolled only men. the first trial of early time-restricted eating, with dinner before 3 pm, followed eight men with prediabetes; its authors wrote that the results "need to be replicated in a larger trial that also includes women".6 another, of a nine-hour window, followed fifteen men.7

trials that did include women often reported them together with men. in TREAT, a widely reported 2020 trial of a 16:8 window, 46 of 116 participants were women, 39.7%. the results were given for the groups as a whole, not for women and men separately.8

pooled reviews show the pattern. a 2026 meta-analysis of 23 randomised trials of a 16:8 window found 6 that enrolled only men, 4 only women, and 13 both. it found lipid improvements only in the studies of men, but most of those men were physically active, and the authors wrote that "the effect of 16/8 TRE is still not clear in the female population".9 a review of Ramadan fasting in healthy people screened 3,870 studies and found 29 that reported differences between women and men.10 a 2026 review of the sex-specific evidence concluded that "direct sex-comparative studies remain scarce".11

where researchers did compare, the differences were small. pooling three twelve-week trials of alternate-day fasting, 75 adults with obesity, weight fell by 4.6% in premenopausal women, 6.5% in postmenopausal women and 6.2% in men, with no significant difference between the groups.12 reviews of trials in women only find modest effects: about 1.9 kg more weight lost with a window than with the comparison diets, across 13 trials and 612 women, though not more than with cutting calories alone;13 and a small fall in BMI and fasting glucose across 22 trials and 1,287 women, with no change in blood pressure, where the fall in BMI held only after one outlying study was left out.14

what have trials in women found about hormones?

less than the worry suggests. a 2022 review of human trials found that fasting didn't change estrogen, the pituitary hormones that drive ovulation, or prolactin in women. androgens such as testosterone fell in premenopausal women with obesity.15 its authors listed their own limits: few studies, samples of 16 to 107 people, hormones measured only as secondary outcomes, many trials without a control group, most of them short, and the women in them all premenopausal. sixth, "it is unclear if the day of the menstrual cycle was recorded and standardized".15

a 2023 trial of eating within four or six hours a day, for eight weeks, in women with obesity found testosterone, androstenedione and SHBG unchanged in 12 premenopausal and 11 postmenopausal women. DHEA, an adrenal hormone, fell by 13 to 14% in both groups.16 a 2026 systematic review found six studies in all. randomised trials in women with obesity found no significant changes in testosterone, estrogen, progesterone, LH, cortisol, DHEA or SHBG; the authors called the evidence inconclusive and asked for longer, more rigorous trials.17

"fasting is bad for women's hormones": is it true?

not on the evidence so far, with one real exception. much of the worry traces to a 2013 study in rats.16 young rats went without food for a full day, every other day, for twelve weeks, and the females' estrous cycles, the rat version of the menstrual cycle, were disrupted, with changes in estradiol and LH.18 the rats were three months old, which the authors of the 2023 trial equate with a human of about nine.16 it says little about an adult woman eating within a window.

in adult women, the trials above found reproductive hormones mostly unchanged. but they were small, short, and mostly in women with obesity, so "unchanged" means "unchanged in those women, over those weeks", not "proven safe for everyone".1517

the real exception is eating too little. ovulation can stop altogether, often with weight loss, stress, heavy exercise or all three; doctors call this functional hypothalamic amenorrhea, and it can lead to bone loss and infertility.19 a 2026 review found that women's reproductive function appears more sensitive than men's to energetic stress, particularly when too little energy is available.11 the risk comes from the shortfall, not from the clock.

"women must cycle sync their fasts": is there evidence?

no trial has tested it. a PubMed search on November 4, 2026 for fasting or time-restricted eating alongside the cycle, cycle syncing, the luteal phase or menstrual phase found no trial that timed fasting to the phases of the cycle.

what exists is indirect. across 15 datasets and 330 women, energy intake was on average 168 kcal a day higher in the luteal phase, the two weeks after ovulation, than in the follicular phase.20 a 2026 review notes that hunger and ghrelin tend to be higher in the luteal phase, and from that reasons that women "may tolerate longer fasting windows more easily" earlier in the cycle.11 that is a reasonable idea, built from studies of appetite, not from studies of fasting. a shorter fast late in the cycle may feel easier. no one has shown that it changes any outcome.

the cycle: the variable most studies don't record

a premenopausal woman measured on day 3 and again on day 22 is, hormonally, being measured twice in two different states. most fasting trials haven't recorded which. in the 2023 trial above, estradiol and progesterone weren't measured in the premenopausal women at all, because "the day of each woman's menstrual cycle was not recorded at time of blood collection".16 the 2022 review couldn't tell whether any trial had standardised it.15

even counting days isn't enough. in a study of 1,060 cycles, 69% of the variation in cycle length came from the follicular phase and 3% from the luteal phase, so a calendar guess at ovulation is often wrong. cycles without ovulation are "not uncommon" even in healthy young women and usually go unnoticed, because the next period still comes.21 research guides recommend confirming ovulation with urine LH tests and checking hormones in blood, rather than assuming a phase from the date.2122

the usual excuse for leaving women out, that their hormones make the data too noisy, doesn't hold up well. pooling seven studies, women's estradiol and progesterone varied far more from visit to visit than men's, but their glucose, insulin, triglycerides, resting metabolic rate and body weight varied no more, with the controls those studies used.23

what women have told researchers

a 2026 review pooled 13 qualitative studies of time-restricted eating, 225 people in all, and looked at women's and men's accounts separately. women more often named disappointment with past diets and dissatisfaction with their bodies as the reason to start, cravings and emotional eating as what got in the way, and better eating habits and control of hunger as what helped.24 one woman of 55, eating from 12 to 6 pm, put it this way: "I've discovered that when I get hungry, I know it's temporary. And if I keep myself busy or just wait a bit longer, that hunger will pass."24

the review doesn't mention the menstrual cycle. whether women find fasting harder at some points of the month is, as far as published studies go, a question nobody has asked them.

what good studies would need

  • enough women, and men, to analyse each separately, planned before the trial starts, as the 1993 law asked for in phase 3 trials.1
  • results reported by sex, even when the numbers are small, so later reviews can pool them.39
  • the cycle recorded at every measurement: the start of the last period, the next one, and ovulation confirmed by LH tests or progesterone, not assumed from the calendar.2122
  • at least three measurements per woman across a cycle, and ideally across two, to see changes within a person.21
  • hormonal contraception, perimenopause and menopause described and treated as separate groups.22
  • periods themselves as an outcome: cycle length, missed periods and ovulation, followed for months, in lean women with regular cycles as well as women with obesity.1517
  • a direct test of fasting by phase against the same hours every day, before anyone says that one is better.

who shouldn't fast, or should ask first

  • anyone with a current or past eating disorder. a clinical review says such patients "should never be encouraged to engage in intermittent fasting".25 a fast that becomes a rule you can't break is a reason to stop.
  • during pregnancy or breastfeeding, in children and teenagers, and in frail older adults, where a 2026 review advises caution or medical supervision.11 the same review calls fasting while trying to conceive "experimental rather than evidence-based care".11
  • anyone with diabetes on insulin or a sulfonylurea. these can cause low blood sugar without food, and their doses need adjusting on fasting days, with a doctor.26
  • anyone whose periods stop or come much further apart. that can go with eating too little,19 and it's a reason to stop fasting and see a doctor.

how hæl handles what isn't known

hæl suggests hours for each phase of the cycle: 12 to 14 while bleeding, 16 to 18 in the follicular phase, 16 to 20 around ovulation, and 14 to 16 in the luteal phase. no trial stands behind those numbers, for the reasons on this page, so hæl treats them as suggestions and nothing more. they show as small marks on the dial, and the dial turns to any length from eight hours.

rest days count as days. there is no coaching: hæl observes, and doesn't tell you what to do. you can use it without a cycle at all. past 48 hours, it says plainly that a fast that long should be medically supervised.

a gentle start in hæl

  1. on the contents page, choose "the first fast". hæl sets the hours to twelve and, when there is still an evening ahead, plans the fast to begin tonight after the last meal, with a note to remind you.
  2. the entry asks for a weight; "a weight, or nothing" is a fair answer. it asks when your last cycle began; give the day, or choose "I don't keep one". you can change either later in settings.
  3. press keep the entry, or begin if the fast starts now. the dial turns, if twelve isn't right.
  4. end when you want to. press end, and hæl asks once, with keep going beside it.
  5. on a day you won't fast, press rest, under begin. the ledger keeps it as rest.
  6. stay at twelve for a while. lengthen slowly, if at all, and shorten when a day asks for it.

the timer, the ledger and rest days are free. the hours for each phase and the readings against the cycle are part of the full witness.

hæl doesn't know more than the research does, and doesn't pretend to. it keeps the hours you choose, suggests without insisting, keeps rest as rest, and works just as well without a cycle.
