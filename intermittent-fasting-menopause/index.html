intermittent fasting in perimenopause and menopause

by the maker of hæl · november 6, 2026

the short answer

in small, short trials, eating within a window led to weight loss after menopause much as it did before it, and some of the loss was muscle. no trial shows that fasting eases hot flushes or helps sleep; hormone therapy remains the most effective treatment for hot flushes, and that is a conversation for a doctor. keep protein up, keep lifting, and with an eating disorder history, on insulin or sulfonylureas, or with low bone density, ask first.

what are perimenopause and menopause?

menopause is the last period, and it can only be named looking back: it counts once there have been twelve months without one. the researchers who wrote the standard staging system, STRAW+10, set out the years around it in stages. the early transition begins when cycles start to vary, with a persistent difference of seven days or more between one cycle and the next. the late transition begins with a gap of 60 days or more without a period, and lasts on average one to three years. after the final period, early postmenopause begins with two stages of about a year each, then a further three to six years, roughly five to eight years in all.1

perimenopause, the word most people use, runs in that system from the early transition to twelve months after the final period.1 in the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN), which followed 3,302 women in the United States, the median age at the final period was 52.54 years.2 the British Menopause Society's summary of the NICE guideline gives the average age as 51.3

NICE advises that blood tests are rarely needed, and should not be used, to diagnose perimenopause or menopause from 45 onwards; FSH, the hormone most often tested, swings too much in these years to say much.3 the staging system doesn't apply to everyone: after a hysterectomy or endometrial ablation there are no periods to stage by, and STRAW+10 says its bleeding criteria don't apply in polycystic ovary syndrome.1

what changes in the body?

SWAN measured 1,246 women by DXA scan, year after year. before the transition, fat mass rose by about 0.25 kg a year. during it, the rate nearly doubled, to 0.45 kg a year, and lean mass began to fall, by about 0.06 kg a year. across the 3.5 years of the transition that came to about 1.6 kg more fat and 0.2 kg less lean mass. weight itself climbed at much the same pace as before, about 0.25 kg a year, so the scale hid most of the change. the shifts settled about two years after the final period.4

bone changes on the same clock. in SWAN, bone loss began a year before the final period and was fastest from then until two years after it. over ten years, the lower spine lost 10.6% of its density, 7.38% of it in those three years, and the hip's femoral neck lost 9.1%.5

hot flushes and night sweats, when frequent, lasted a median of 7.4 years in SWAN, and went on for a median of 4.5 years after the final period.6 in a SWAN survey of 12,603 women aged 40 to 55, 38% reported trouble sleeping in the past two weeks, rising to 45.4% in the late transition.7

what have fasting trials found?

the first studies came from a group in Chicago that compared women before and after menopause within the same trials. all were in adults with obesity, and all left out women in perimenopause, because those years bring metabolic changes of their own.89

  • eight weeks, eating within four to six hours a day: 13 premenopausal and 19 postmenopausal women both lost 3.3% of their weight, and kept to the window about 6.2 days a week. the postmenopausal women lost about 2.1 kg of fat and 1.0 kg of lean mass. there was no comparison group that didn't fast.8
  • twelve weeks of alternate day fasting, 500 calories on fasting days: 43 premenopausal and 22 postmenopausal women lost 4.6% and 6.5% of their weight, with no significant difference between them. everyone ate 400 to 500 calories more than planned on fasting days.9
  • hormones in the same eight-week trial: estradiol, estrone and progesterone, measured only after menopause, didn't change. DHEA, an adrenal hormone, fell by about 13%.10 over twelve months, in 90 adults, an eight-hour window from noon to 8 pm left testosterone, DHEA, SHBG and, after menopause, the estrogens and progesterone unchanged, while weight fell compared with a control group.11

newer trials take in a wider span of midlife:

  • twelve weeks, 104 women aged 40 to 60 in Hong Kong, including women before, during and after the transition: an eight-hour window, aerobic exercise, both, or neither. against no change, the window alone lowered fat mass by 1.29 kg, and the window with exercise by 2.85 kg. the authors note that the mix of menopausal stages, and small numbers in each, limit what the subgroups can say.12
  • twelve weeks, 62 women aged about 51 in Poland: a 16:8 window with circuit training, against the training alone. BMI and systolic blood pressure fell more with the window. groups were not randomised: some women were allocated in turn, others chose their group.13
  • eight weeks, 142 postmenopausal women aged 50 to 60 in China: an eight-hour window with or without Tai Chi, against usual life. cholesterol and a measure of blood vessel function improved most with both together.14
  • in the Polish trial, adding the window to exercise brought no extra benefit on tests of memory and attention.15

read together: after menopause, eating within a window led to weight loss about as large as it did before menopause, over weeks to a few months. some of what was lost was lean mass. the trials are small and short, most are in women with higher weight, and the longest ran a year. none was designed to show what happens across the whole transition.

what about muscle?

any way of eating less tends to take some muscle with the fat, and in the Chicago trials about a third of the weight lost after menopause was lean mass.89 in a 2026 trial of 100 postmenopausal women, every group did Nordic walking. those who also kept a ten-hour window and ate about 1.4 g of protein per kilogram of body weight a day gained 1.19 kg of lean mass in the arms and legs over twelve weeks. those who kept the window without the protein target gained 0.04 kg, about none, and lost a little in the legs.16 the authors say the result compares packages, not single parts.

for people over 65, an expert group recommends at least 1.0 to 1.2 g of protein per kilogram a day, and 1.2 g or more for those who exercise.17 that was written for older adults, not for menopause in particular, but it points the same way: a shorter eating window still needs enough protein inside it.

and bone?

the fasting evidence on bone is thin. in a six-month trial of alternate day fasting in 100 adults, bone density and markers of bone turnover didn't change, and they didn't differ between women before and after menopause.18 we found no fasting trial in women with osteoporosis, and none that followed bone after menopause for longer than six months.

what is better known is that losing weight can cost bone. in a year-long trial in adults over 65 with obesity, those who lost weight by diet alone lost 3% of their hip bone density and 5% of their lean mass. those who also exercised lost 1% and 3%.19 that trial wasn't about fasting, or only about women, but the lesson carries: eating less without moving more asks more of the skeleton.

exercise has the stronger evidence. in the LIFTMOR trial, 101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass did eight months of supervised heavy resistance and impact training, twice a week for 30 minutes. their lower spine density rose 2.9%, against a fall of 1.2% in the low-intensity group, and one adverse event was reported.20 that was under close supervision; heavy lifting with low bone density is something to start with a professional.

hot flushes and sleep

no trial has tested fasting with hot flushes as its main outcome. the nearest is the Polish trial: 54 women finished twelve weeks of training, with or without a 16:8 window. total menopause symptom scores fell more in the window group, but there was no difference in the vasomotor part, the hot flushes and night sweats, and no change in sleep quality. groups were partly chosen by the women themselves, a third dropped out, and the authors list selection bias among the limits.21

in the Hong Kong trial, sleep quality didn't change in any group. six women in the two window groups reported hunger that may have affected their sleep in the first two weeks, which eased as they adapted.12 a 2026 systematic review of diets for menopausal symptoms found the evidence for intermittent fasting limited.22

so there is no evidence that fasting eases hot flushes or helps sleep in menopause. the Menopause Society's 2023 statement on treatments other than hormones recommends cognitive behavioural therapy, clinical hypnosis, some prescription medicines and, at a lower level of evidence, weight loss, and does not recommend dietary modification for hot flushes.23

hormone therapy is a conversation for a doctor

the Menopause Society (then the North American Menopause Society) says in its 2022 statement that hormone therapy remains the most effective treatment for hot flushes and night sweats, and has been shown to prevent bone loss and fracture. for those under 60, or within ten years of menopause, without reasons not to take it, it judges the balance of benefit and risk favourable. the risks depend on the type, dose, route, timing and length of use.24

NICE, in England, says hormone replacement therapy should be offered for hot flushes and sweats after the benefits and risks have been weighed, and that menopause-specific cognitive behavioural therapy can be considered alongside it, or instead of it, including for sleep broken by night sweats. it also suggests advice on diet and lifestyle, such as stopping smoking, weight loss, less alcohol and more exercise.3

none of that is something a timer can decide. if symptoms are wearing you down, a doctor can go through the options with you, and fasting is no substitute for that conversation.

who shouldn't fast, or should ask first

  • anyone with a current or past eating disorder. they don't stop at 40: in a survey of 715 Austrian women aged 40 to 60, 4.6% met full criteria for an eating disorder, mostly binge eating disorder and related forms.25 a fast that turns into a rule you can't break is a reason to stop.
  • anyone with diabetes on insulin or a sulfonylurea, such as glipizide, glimepiride or glyburide. these can cause low blood sugar when you don't eat, and an expert review advises lower doses on fasting days, with a doctor's guidance; metformin can usually be continued.26 don't change a dose on your own.
  • anyone with osteoporosis, low bone density or a past fracture from a small fall. there is no fasting trial to go on, and weight loss can cost bone.19
  • anyone on or starting hormone therapy who has bleeding they didn't expect. NICE advises seeking help promptly for unscheduled bleeding after the first three months.3
  • anyone who bleeds after menopause, even a small amount or only once. the NHS advises seeing a GP.27 it isn't a new cycle to log; it's a reason to be checked.

what if your periods are irregular, or have stopped?

then hours suggested for each phase of the cycle may not fit you. in the late transition, gaps of two months or more are part of the definition,1 and ovulation doesn't follow a calendar. no trial has tested fasting by phase in anyone, and in perimenopause there is nothing at all to go on. after menopause there is no cycle to follow.

hæl counts the phase from the day your last period began and the cycle length it knows, which it keeps between 21 and 45 days. if a period comes later than that, it counts on as though a new cycle had begun, so until you enter the next period, the phase on screen is a guess. when gaps grow long, the guess is often wrong, and turning the cycle off is the more honest choice.

without a cycle, hæl doesn't invent one. the ledger draws the month by the moon, with your fasts round it, and home shows the moon's phase under the dial. the moon is only a calendar there, a way to see a month at a glance; hæl makes no claim that it moves the body, or that it has anything to do with menopause.

setting up hæl in perimenopause or after menopause

  1. on the first page, hæl asks when your last cycle began. if periods still come, give the day. if they've stopped, or come too rarely to count, choose "I don't keep one". you can change either later, in settings, under the cycle.
  2. if you keep a cycle through perimenopause, enter each period when it comes: in settings, change the date of your last period, or log it in Apple Health and hæl reads it from there.
  3. when the gaps grow long, or a year has passed without a period, turn off "track a cycle" in settings, under the cycle. the ring keeps the hours alone.
  4. start at twelve hours. turn the dial to twelve and press begin. lengthen slowly, if at all, and keep enough protein in the hours you eat.
  5. keep rest days. under begin is rest. press it on a day you won't fast, and the ledger keeps the day as rest rather than a gap.
  6. read the scale slowly. without a cycle, the body ledger draws the last 28 days against your usual weight over the last 90, with the month before beside it. in these years the scale can stay flat while fat and muscle shift underneath,4 so the trend over months says more than any morning.
