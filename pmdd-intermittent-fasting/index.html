PMDD and intermittent fasting

by the maker of hæl · november 6, 2026

the short answer

PMDD is a severe sensitivity to the normal hormone changes before a period, confirmed in about 3 in 100 people who menstruate. no trial has tested intermittent fasting in PMDD. appetite and cravings rise in those days, more so with PMDD, and PMDD is linked with binge eating. a shorter fast, or rest, then is a reasonable choice; treatment is for a doctor.

hæl, a fasting timer for iPhone, suggests shorter fasts late in the cycle and keeps a day of rest as rest, never as a gap.

what is PMDD?

premenstrual dysphoric disorder is a severe, recurring change in mood that comes in the days before a period and lifts soon after it starts. it isn't a hormone imbalance. hormone levels in PMDD are normal; what differs is the brain's response to their ordinary rise and fall. in a well-known trial, switching the ovaries off eased symptoms in women with premenstrual syndrome, and adding back normal doses of estrogen or progesterone brought the symptoms back, while the same hormones changed nothing in women without it.1 the International Association for Premenstrual Disorders describes PMDD in the same terms, as a sensitivity to normal hormonal change.2

how is it diagnosed?

in outline, the DSM-5, the psychiatric manual most doctors use, asks for all of these:3

  • at least five symptoms in the final week before the period, in most cycles of the past year, improving within a few days of the period starting and minimal or absent in the week after it.
  • at least one of them a mood symptom: sudden swings of mood, marked irritability or anger, low mood or hopelessness, or marked anxiety or tension.
  • the rest from a list that includes losing interest in usual things, poor concentration, low energy, a marked change in appetite, overeating or food cravings, sleeping too much or too little, feeling overwhelmed, and physical symptoms such as tender breasts or bloating.
  • real distress, or interference with work, study, relationships or daily life.
  • symptoms confirmed by daily ratings over at least two cycles. until then, the diagnosis is called provisional.
  • not simply another condition, such as depression, getting worse before a period, and not caused by a medicine or another illness.

appetite is written into the definition. that matters for anyone who fasts.

how common is PMDD?

less common than headlines suggest, and the number depends on how it is counted. a 2024 meta-analysis of 44 studies and 50,659 participants found 3.2% when the diagnosis was confirmed by daily ratings, and 7.7% when it was provisional. in community samples with confirmed diagnosis, it was 1.6%.4 the authors conclude that studies without daily ratings overstate it.

it is serious. in a global survey of 599 people with PMDD confirmed by daily ratings, 72% reported having had active thoughts of suicide at some point in their lives, and 34% a suicide attempt.5 that survey recruited through PMDD support groups, so it may lean toward the most affected, but the figures are why PMDD is treated as a medical condition, not a bad week.

if PMDD brings thoughts of suicide or self-harm, that is an emergency. call your local emergency number, or 988 in the US, or Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK.

what do doctors recommend?

the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists and the UK's primary care guidance agree on the main treatments.678

  • SSRIs, a kind of antidepressant, taken every day or only in the second half of the cycle. a 2024 Cochrane review of 34 trials found they probably reduce premenstrual symptoms, more so when taken every day, with nausea, tiredness and sleepiness the commonest side effects.9
  • cognitive behavioural therapy. ACOG recommends it for the mood symptoms, and the RCOG says it should be considered routinely in severe PMS.67
  • hormonal options, such as some combined contraceptive pills, and, for severe symptoms under a specialist, medicines that pause the cycle altogether.68

these are things to talk through with a doctor. no diet or fasting schedule is among them.

has intermittent fasting been studied in PMDD?

no. as of november 2026, searches of PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov found no trial of intermittent fasting, time-restricted eating or meal timing in people with PMDD. there is no evidence that fasting helps PMDD, and none that it harms it. there is simply no study.

the nearest is a trial in premenstrual syndrome, which is milder and more common. sixty women with overweight or obesity ate very little every other day, or cut calories daily, for eight weeks. the fasting group improved more on some measures, mood swings and expressed anger among them, but they also lost weight, the trial was open-label, and almost a fifth dropped out.10 a 2026 review of intermittent eating in women noted that weight loss on these diets has improved premenstrual symptoms, and found no evidence of an effect apart from the weight loss.11 neither tells us anything about PMDD in someone who isn't trying to lose weight.

what happens to appetite before a period?

it rises, a little, for most women. a 2024 meta-analysis of 15 datasets found energy intake higher in the luteal phase, the two weeks after ovulation, than in the follicular phase, by about 168 kcal a day on average.12 the studies were in women of healthy weight with no history of disordered eating, and the range between women was wide.

in PMDD the rise is larger. in a laboratory study, women with PMDD, but not women without it, wanted more high-fat food in the late luteal days and ate more calories then.13 in a study of 62 women with PMDD and 69 without, those with PMDD had bigger late-luteal increases in calories, in sweet calories, and in eating that felt out of control.14 in another, women with PMDD, but not the others, wanted sweet food more in the late luteal phase, and that desire tracked their irritability and impulsivity.15

and blood sugar?

it shifts slightly. in 49 young women without diabetes who wore glucose monitors for about eleven weeks, daily glucose was lowest in the late follicular phase, about 5.8 mmol/L, and highest in the luteal phase, about 6.1 mmol/L, peaking around day 24. days with more food cravings had slightly higher glucose.16 these are small changes inside the normal range.

the old idea that premenstrual symptoms are low blood sugar has not held up. two small studies gave glucose tolerance tests at both ends of the cycle in women with premenstrual syndrome and found no cycle-related difference in how they handled glucose, and symptoms during the tests did not match their premenstrual symptoms.1718 no study has measured blood sugar during a fast in PMDD.

one more thing worth knowing: UK guidance for premenstrual syndrome advises regular, frequent, small meals, every two to three hours, rich in complex carbohydrates.8 it is general lifestyle advice, not tested against fasting, but it points the other way from long gaps between meals.

PMDD, binge eating and fasting

the links are real and worth taking seriously. in a nationally representative US sample of 8,694 women, PMDD was linked with about seven times the odds of bulimia nervosa, after accounting for other mental health conditions. a link with binge-eating disorder was seen too, but it did not hold once those conditions were accounted for.19 the study was cross-sectional, so it can't say which comes first.

in women in general, emotional eating rises and falls with the cycle and is highest in the middle of the luteal phase, when progesterone peaks.20 and going without food matters: in 496 adolescent girls followed for five years, fasting for a day to control weight predicted later binge eating and bulimia more strongly than ordinary dieting did.21 that was full-day fasting for weight control, in teenagers, not a daily overnight window in adults, but the lesson carries: restriction and bingeing tend to feed each other.

put together: in the very days when PMDD raises hunger and cravings, a long fast asks for the most restraint. for someone with a history of disordered eating, that is a reason not to fast at all. for everyone else, it is a reason to make the late luteal days the easiest of the month, not the hardest.

what women have told researchers

only a few studies have asked. in an Australian study of women who felt worse about their bodies before a period, many described strict eating the rest of the month, broken premenstrually by hunger and cravings. one said she would "crave food all night and that's not normally me". another said, "I'm eating more than I should and I know that I shouldn't eat so much, but I do it anyway". some gave themselves permission, "I say it could be my hormones and that's the reason, it's okay", while others described guilt; one said, "I feel disgusted with my lack of self-control."22 the study was about premenstrual body dissatisfaction, not PMDD diagnosed by daily ratings.

in a UK study of 17 women diagnosed with PMDD by a specialist, symptoms had begun at a median age of 15 and diagnosis came at a median age of 35. one woman said of the diagnosis, "It made everything make sense and everything just clicked into place". another described her untreated years as "self-destruction and anxiety and eating disorders".23

who shouldn't fast

with or without PMDD, some people should not fast without a doctor's advice:

  • with a current or past eating disorder, including bingeing or purging
  • during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or while trying to conceive
  • with diabetes, or on medicines that lower blood sugar
  • under 18
  • with a missed or irregular period that hasn't been looked into
  • when fasting starts to feel like a way to punish or control yourself, or like something you can't stop

if you are being treated for PMDD, mention fasting to whoever prescribes for you.

a shorter fast, or none, in the late luteal days

if you fast and live with PMDD or strong premenstrual symptoms, shortening the fast, or not fasting, in the last week before a period is a reasonable personal choice. it follows from what is known: hunger and cravings rise then, more so in PMDD, and restraint is hardest when mood is lowest. it isn't a treatment. no study shows that a shorter fast, or any fast, eases PMDD, and nothing here should replace the treatments above.

a rest day in those days isn't a lapse. it is the practice working as it should.
